## Q1. How were the bores drilled?

**Short answer.** They were drilled by **percussion**. A walking beam driven by a steam engine lifted a heavy steel chisel and dropped it, over and over. It was not rotary drilling. In Queensland the usual system was the **"Canadian" pole-tool system**, in which the bit hangs from a string of screwed-together **wooden rods (poles)**. The **American rope (cable) system** was also used, especially for the deepest part of very deep bores. Diamond (rotary core) drills were tried and rejected for this country. Hydraulic jetting was offered for sinking tubes through quicksand. Hydraulic *rotary* drilling does not appear in any 1890s Queensland source I found.

Evidence:

- Cox says the choice narrows to one family: "The question then becomes narrowed down to the acceptance of what is known as the American, or Canadian, system ... The difference of detail consists in the use of a cable for drilling in one mode, and the use of poles for the same purpose in the other." [Cox 1895, pp. 103-104]
- On the pole system: "Under the pole method stout poles of wood in long lengths of from 16 to 20 feet with screwed wrought-iron ends are used in lieu of a cable." [Cox 1895, p. 104]
- Cox names the principle (the lift-and-drop beam, as opposed to rotation): "the substitution of a balanced beam ... resting upon a middle bearing to one end of which the cable or poles with the chisel attached are suspended, the other end being given an upward and downward movement by means of a crank movement worked by a steam engine in place of a rotary movement as in the diamond drill". [Cox 1895, p. 111]
- Cox is sceptical of cable-only drilling at depth: "This mode of drilling by impact alone, although assisted by a twisting action of the cable, will never, I am of opinion, become general for artesian or deep boring." [Cox 1895, p. 104]
- **Who ran the rigs:** "the services have been enlisted of Americans, mostly Canadians from British North America". [Cox 1895, p. 112] So "Canadian Bill" is true to type. No specific person of that name is known.
- **First Queensland bores:**
  - Blackall (Government) was started in January 1886 "but it was abandoned waterless at a depth of about 1000 feet, the cable system which had been used in drilling it proving unsuitable." [Cox 1895, p. 52]
  - Barcaldine: "The tools to sink it with were landed from Canada in July, 1887. The machinery was made principally in Sydney." [Cox 1895, p. 52]
- **Deep bores switched to rope:**
  - At Malvern Hills "the contractor found the pole system impracticable, with his plant, at a greater depth than 3650 feet, and a 2in. Manilla rope cable was then employed to a depth of 3948 feet." [Cox 1895, p. 104 fn.]
  - At Bimerah (5,045 ft, the deepest in Australia by 1906), "a cable was used to complete the work." [Cox 1906, p. 104]
- **Plants became local:** "Boring plants were at first imported from Canada and from Germany ... Complete plants are now supplied by Brisbane, Rockhampton and Townsville manufacturing firms." [Cox 1895, p. 54] In 1906 Cox says any foundry "such as those at Adelaide, Sydney, Brisbane, and Rockhampton" built plants, and that contractors argued hotly over "the particular make, size, and working of rival machines". [Cox 1906, p. 101]
- **Diamond drill:** "It has had a full and fair trial in the alluvial ... Cretaceous ... country, but without sufficient success to secure its adoption." [Cox 1906, p. 95] It was "worse than useless" in clay and sand. [Cox 1895, p. 102]
- **NSW** first tried augers (Tiffin; Wright and Edwards) and found them "inadequate". It then copied Queensland's contract system. [Otago Witness, 14 Jul 1892, summarising J. W. Boultbee's NSW report. https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18920714.2.12]

**Impact, stroke and speed.**
- For the American rope rig, Cox (quoting his own 1879 article) describes the drill "striking from thirty to forty blows per minute". [Cox 1895, p. 103] Isler says the same, adding "The fall of the tools is 2 to 3 ft." [Isler 1911, pp. 145-146]
- For the Canadian rig, Boyd writes: "In the rig used, the fall allowed was 18½ inches." The jar links "must be longer than the stroke of the engine beam, and in the Canadian rig they are generally 2 feet 6 inches in length." Also: "In good drilling ground it is not exceptional to run at the rate of 60 and even 70 strokes a minute, either with wooden rods or ropes." [Boyd 1894, pp. 120-125]
- Galician practice ranged from "short (3-in.) strokes at high speed (140 strokes per minute)" to "long (20-in.) strokes at a maximum speed of 60 per minute". [Isler 1911, p. 152]
- The stroke was adjustable. The band-wheel crank has "several holes, in either of which ... according to the length of stroke of the walking beam desired ... is inserted a wrist pin". [Cox 1895, p. 105]
- **Summary for Queensland: roughly 40-70 blows a minute, with the bit falling 1½ to 3 ft.** No Queensland-specific stroke figure was found (**UNVERIFIED** for Queensland in particular).

**How the bit was turned between blows.**
- **Pole system.** The poles are rigid, so the driller turns the whole string from the top. Cox: the poles "from their rigidity ... admit of certain and trustworthy turning of the chisel at its work in the bottom of the bore-hole". [Cox 1895, p. 107]
- The string hangs from the beam on "an attachment screw fixed on a swivel ... The swivel enables the driller to turn the tools round ... in order to keep the hole round and the bottom level." [Boyd 1894, p. 124]
- When asked whether the tools were "turned simply by muscular power", Boyd replied that at the top of the stroke "there was a moment when, irrespective of the weight, the whole set of rods and tools ... could be turned round by means of a swivel at the top of the rods. A very small turn was quite sufficient. It was not necessary to turn the tool round at each stroke when the machine was going at from 60 to 70 strokes a minute." [Boyd 1894, discussion, p. 139]
- Thompson: pole tools suit caving ground where it is "necessary to have some positive method of rotating the bit", and "the rotation of the rods at the surface assur[es] the twisting of the bit in the hole." [Thompson 1910, pp. 193, 196]
- **Rope system.** "Between the strokes the tools require to be moved around to make the aperture uniform and to prevent them from wedging fast." [Cox 1895, p. 103] "One man is required constantly in the derrick, to turn the tools as they rise and fall ... and to let out the temper-screw as required." [Isler 1911, p. 145] Cox's 1906 kit list includes a "Wood handle for turning tools". [Cox 1906, p. 99]

## Q2. The rig, part by part

The fullest Queensland source is Cox's machinery chapter and his App. C, "Artesian Well Boring Tools and Plant Complete", which is effectively a shopping list for a 2,000 ft Canadian outfit. Boyd and Thompson explain the Canadian-specific parts: the spring pole, the slipper-out and the cow-sucker.

| Part | What it is, with size (source) |
|---|---|
| **Derrick** | A tall tapering four-legged tower directly over the bore. It "may be constructed of timber or of angle and flat iron". A full-size timber derrick is "20ft. square at the base and 72ft. high, the four corners converging so as to form a square at the top 3ft. in diameter, upon which rests a heavy framework for the reception of the crown pulley" [Cox 1895, p. 104]. Cox's own diagram is marked 47 ft (`ref/01`). Canadian rigs in Austria were 56 ft [Boyd 1894, p. 124], and Thompson's specification is "56 by 18 feet, square at base" [Thompson 1910, p. 197]. The rig stands on sills: "six foundation posts, each 18in. square", with floor sills and planks "with a hole in centre for the drill to pass through" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. It has ladders and two stagings "for the workmen to attach and detach the lengths of drill poles or tubing" [Cox 1895, p. 110]. "The height of the derrick admits of three lengths of rods being lifted at a time without unscrewing them" [Cox 1895, p. 107], so poles come up in stands of three (roughly 50-60 ft). Photos show lattice iron derricks (Marathon, Barenya, Charleville district), timber ones (Leichhardt) and single guyed masts (Saxby Downs; `ref/03`-`06`). |
| **Samson post** | "made of timber 18in. by 20in. at the bottom, and 18in. square at the top, and 13ft. high", dovetailed into the main sill and braced [Cox 1895, p. 105]. It carries the saddle bearing on which the beam rocks. |
| **Walking beam** | "of hard wood, 26ft. long, 12in. by 26in. at its middle, where it rests on the saddle or samson post ... At its front end a slot is cut, in which the drilling hook is hung" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. Cox's diagram calls it the rocking beam. It runs from over the hole, inside the derrick, out to the crank. |
| **Band wheel, crank and pitman** | The band wheel "is 9ft. in diameter. It is connected with the pulley of the engine by a belt". Its shaft carries a crank with a wrist pin, "to which is attached ... the 'pitman,' which is connected by a stirrup with the walking beam. The rotation of the band wheel therefore causes a rocking motion of the walking beam" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. In Boyd's Canadian rig the band wheel "is about 5 times the diameter of the pulley" on the engine flywheel shaft [Boyd 1894, p. 124], so the engine turns several times for each stroke of the beam. |
| **Spring pole and slipper-out** (Canadian feed) | "On the other end of the walking beam is fixed a spring pole sustaining the drilling tools, by means of a short piece of chain ... wound round a reel fixed on the walking beam about the position of its fulcrum ... The reel ... enables the chain to be payed out as the boring descends, by means of a 'slipper out,' which is a combination of pawl and ratchet acted on by a string at the command of the driller" [Boyd 1894, p. 124]. Thompson says the same, with Fig. 51 labelling "Spring Pole" and "Slipper Out" (`ref/02`) [Thompson 1910, p. 194]. The Queensland plant list includes "1 cast iron spring pole-jacket", "1 slipper-out, complete (steel wheel)", "1 stirrup and king bolt for spring pole" and a "hardwood walking beam and spring pole" [Cox 1895, App. C]. |
| **Temper screw** (rope rigs) | Hung on the drilling hook. It "is gradually let out as the bit cuts into the rock ... The screw is 5½ft. long and 1¼in. in diameter, with a square thread of two threads to the inch" [Cox 1895, p. 106; the diameter digit is uncertain in the OCR]. Isler's Fig. 79 shows one. On pole rigs the slipper-out does this job. |
| **Hoisting drums (draw spool, "bull wheel")** | Cox's diagram: "The winding drum is at E beside the rocking beam, and is driven by a belt ... A wire rope from the winding drum passes over the pulley G at the top of the derrick ending in the hook H immediately over the bore-hole" [Cox 1895, p. 110]. App. C lists "1 draw spool ... and 1 sand line spool, with brakes; 2 belt tighteners". Spools were thrown into gear by levers pressing "jockey pulleys" against slack belts [Thompson 1910, p. 194]. To hoist, the beam is disconnected and "the driller, by the aid of a brake, can control the appliance" [Boyd 1894, p. 124]. The rope ends in "a heavy cast-iron weight called a 'cow-sucker'", which pulls the empty rope back down [Boyd 1894, p. 125]. App. C lists "1 cowsucker and shackles". |
| **Sand reel, sand pump and bailer** | The sand line is coiled on its own drum K and runs over its own pulley O at the derrick top [Cox 1895, p. 110]. Bailers are "made of wrought iron 20ft. long. One has a bail at the top and a foot valve at the bottom. The valve stem projects downwards below the pump, so that the valve may be opened and the contents of the pump discharged by resting the stem on the ground" [Cox 1895, p. 106]. App. C lists "2000 ft. Bullivant's steel-wire rope for sand line" and sand pumps of 4, 5 and 6½ in "with brass valve and seat". |
| **Rods ("yellow rods")** | "The Poles. These are made of tough light timber (preferably of Queensland yellow-wood or Crow's ash) of a circular form, 3in. or 4in. in diameter, and in 16ft. to 20ft. lengths. They have wrought-iron taper-screwed ends for coupling and uncoupling" [Cox 1895, pp. 106-107]. App. C: "2000 ft. 2½ in. best drill-poles" and "60 sets pole joints and centre straps". **Why yellow:** Queensland yellow-wood (*Flindersia oxleyana*) has "Wood of a pale yellow colour, and a distinctive odour. Chief uses for coach-building, railway-carriage frame-work, **boring rods**" (Queensland Forestry Museum catalogue 1904, quoted in [Maiden], vol. 2, No. 74). The species name *xanthoxyla* means "yellow wood", from "the remarkable yellow colour of the wood" (Hooker 1830, quoted there). Another quote there says it "quite fills the place of English and American ash", the timber the original Canadian poles were made from ("ash rods" [Isler 1911, p. 158]; "wooden ash drill poles" [Thompson 1910, p. 194]). Thompson gives the Canadian standard: "2 to 2½ inches hexagonal or round trimmed ash, in lengths of about 18 feet, to the extremities of which are riveted iron straps which partially encircle the rod and have a screwed pin or a box joint." Two can be "coupled together by means of two iron straps encircling the pole" [Thompson 1910, p. 196]. Poles are light when wet: the bore "is generally full of water from the upper springs", and wooden poles "lose a great deal of their weight in the water" [Cox 1895, p. 107]. That is the "water down below" of the poem. |
| **Jars** | "made in two parts, and are like two long links of a chain. Both parts are slotted, and the cross-head of one passes through the slot of the other. When extended the jars are 6ft. long, and when closed 5ft. 3in. The difference, 9in., is the play of the jars. The function of the jars is to give a blow upward" to free a stuck bit [Cox 1895, p. 106]. With jars under the poles, "the chisel, or bit, alone strikes the rock, the poles above it falling a less distance than the play" [Cox 1895, p. 107]. |
| **Sinker bar / stem** | Heavy iron bar above the bit, giving it weight. Cox's rope string has a "sinker bar 12ft. long, weighing 400lb" and an "auger stem 32ft. long, weighing 1050lb" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. The Queensland pole outfit lists sinker bars 2½-4 in thick, "30 ft. long, joint in centre" [App. C]. |
| **Bit (chisel)** | A steel chisel, "3ft. 4in. long, weighing 140lb" (rope string), and a "rimer 3ft. 4in. long, weighing 160lbs., for making the hole round" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. App. C lists 5, 6, 8 and 10-in bits and an "under-runner for 6-in. and 8-in. casing" (an under-reamer, which cuts the hole wider than the casing so the casing can follow). |
| **Dressing bits (forge)** | "When some 12in. of rock have been cut the drill and rods are raised ... The bit is unscrewed and is dressed or sharpened, carefully cut to the size of the gauge, and one is screwed on ready to drill again" [Cox 1895, p. 108]. App. C's blacksmith kit: "1 blacksmith's anvil, 6 pairs blacksmith's tongs, 1 large fuller, 1 hot chisel, 1 set swedges ... 1 blacksmith's bellows ... 1 large flatter, 1 forge complete ... 3 steel sledge hammers". Isler's plates show a ring-shaped "chisel gauge". |
| **Engine and boiler** | "generally a portable one of not less than 15 horse-power, having a cylinder at least 9in. by 12in. ... securely anchored on the ground", with power carried by belt to the band wheel [Cox 1895, p. 105]. The App. C outfit has a cylinder "10 in. x 12 in. (equal to 25 h.p., American Catalogue)", a steel boiler "tested for a working pressure of 60 lb. per square inch", and a "governor specially designed for well-boring purposes". The driller controls the engine from the derrick: "the throttle valve of the engine has a large grooved wheel attached to it, and from this grooved wheel the endless cord, called the 'telegraph', extends to the derrick and passes round another grooved wheel, which is fastened to a post convenient to the driller" [Cox 1895, p. 105]. Photos show wheeled portables with a big flywheel and a tall stack beside the rig (`ref/03`-`05`). **Maker:** no Glasgow-built boring engine was found (**UNVERIFIED**). "Twenty horse-power" is in the right range: Cox wants at least 15 nominal hp, and his list gives 25. |
| **Casing ("tubes")** | "of wrought iron screwed together, with 'swelled joints' to admit of deeper screwing, and are 'flush' inside, that is, free from any projections" [Cox 1895, p. 110]. The strings telescope: a big drive-pipe to rock, then smaller tubes inside. At Barcaldine "The bore ... was 1ft in diameter down to 320 ft, at which depth salt water was struck, and iron tubing was placed; beyond that, the bore was 9in." [NZ Herald, 14 Feb 1889, https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZH18890214.2.52]. At Bimerah (1897) "The well has been cased with Stewart's artesian casing for a depth of 3956 feet. The bore has the longest string of 5 inch casing ever put into the one sinking" [Nelson Evening Mail, 15 Feb 1897, https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NEM18970215.2.21.3.12]. "Stewart's" is very probably A. & J. Stewart of Glasgow (tube works opened in Glasgow in 1860; *The Engineer*, 11 Dec 1953, https://archive.org/details/sim_engineer_1953-12-11_196_5107). If so, **the Scottish connection in the poem is true of the tubes** even if not of the engine (an inference). The casing tools in App. C: "casing dogs", "steel casing shoes, 5 in., 6 in., and 8 in.", "casing chain ring, and hook", "51 pairs iron casing clamps and bolts", "1 improved new design automatic casing clamp". |
| **Freeing and forcing tubes** | App. C includes "2 hydraulic jacks", "2 treble iron blocks, 10-in." and heavy chain. Isler shows hollow hydraulic jacks for withdrawing tubes [Isler 1911, pp. 65-67]. In the Boyd discussion, casing in clay was "put down with the help of the walking beam". Another speaker preferred a wooden ram hung on the sand line with heavy iron clamps, and warned: "if in lowering the string of casing in clay the work was stopped say every night, it might be impossible to move the casing again the next morning" [Boyd 1894, p. 133]. A drive-pipe was driven with "a heavy maul made of a sound log of timber 15ft. long ... lifted through the crank movement by the engine" [Cox 1895, p. 108]. |
| **Fishing tools** | For broken or stuck strings: "1 pair spring grabs; 1 spud, 8 ft. long", two-legged and one-legged sockets, and "1 half-turn hook" [App. C]. |
| **Derrick-floor tools** | "2 knock wrenches; 2 catch wrenches; 1 pair heavy tool wrenches; 1 bit lever and chains; 1 extra large iron wrench board and stool; 1 pole-holder; 1 joint clamp" [App. C]. Poles are rested on the "wrench block, a piece of timber covered by sheet iron" while joints are broken [Boyd 1894, p. 125]. |
| **Lighting** | "3 hurricane lanterns, 1 12-in. engine room lamp and reflector, 2 dozen lampwicks ... 2 dozen lamp chimneys" [App. C]. Work went on at night. |
| **Belts** | "70 ft. best 12-in. leather belting for draw belts, 45 ft. best 12 in. indiarubber for driving-belts" [App. C]. The drive belt is therefore roughly 20 ft long, which puts the engine a few yards from the band wheel. |

**Site layout (how the parts sit).** Read Cox's diagram (`ref/01`) and Thompson's Fig. 51 (`ref/02`) together with the photos:

- **Derrick over the hole**, with the bore mouth in the middle of its plank floor.
- **Walking beam** starts over the hole inside the derrick. It runs out horizontally through one side of the derrick to the **samson post**, roughly halfway along, which is its pivot. It carries on to the **crank end** above the **band wheel**.
- **Hoisting drums** sit on the rig frame beside the beam and are belt-driven from the band-wheel shaft. Ropes run from them up to the crown pulleys.
- The **portable engine** stands beyond the band wheel on the same line, belted to it, with its **stack** standing up.
- Firewood is stacked near the engine; Barenya shows a big woodpile (`ref/05`).
- In the open around the rig: poles and pipes lying in racks or piles, and coils of wire rope (`ref/03`).
- The **forge**, anvil and water casks or tanks sit nearby.
- The camp is further off: "artesian borers' camp" is Paterson's own phrase (below).

## Q3. The crew, and what the work looked like

**Crew list.** "The men required for working it are one engine driver, one driller and one derrick man, if near a township; but if in the far away bush a cook and blacksmith are also required, making in all five men. The Canadian drillers work twelve hours a day, and when a double set of men is employed the work goes on day and night." [Cox 1895, p. 110]

Roles, as the sources describe them:

- **Driller**: in charge of the hole. He stands at the bore mouth on the derrick floor.
  - His hand is on the top of the string at the swivel. At the top of the stroke he gives it "a very small turn" [Boyd 1894].
  - He works the **slipper-out string** to feed the tools down [Boyd 1894, p. 124].
  - He has the **telegraph cord** to the engine throttle on a post beside him [Cox 1895, p. 105].
  - He feels the bottom through the string. In the discussion: "As he could not see his work, he must be able to feel". Boyd answered that the blow is "carried to the end of the driller, quite as delicately through the ash pole of the Canadian system, as through the hemp rope" [Boyd 1894, pp. 133, 140].
  - On a rope rig "The driller takes his seat on a high stool above the chosen spot" [Cox 1895, pp. 102-103, quoting his 1879 description of American practice].
- **Derrick man**: works up on the staging. "As the poles are drawn up they are received and detached by a workman at the top platform of the derrick, while the driller at the ground level rests them on the wrench block" [Boyd 1894, p. 125]. The Marathon and Barenya photos show men on the derrick stagings and at the crown (`ref/03`, `ref/05`).
- **Engine driver / fireman** ("Canadian Bill is firing"): keeps steam in a wood-fired portable, minds water and gauges, and answers the telegraph.
  - The station owner, not the contractor, found the fuel and water: the owner "will find provide and convey ... all necessary casing ... and all wood coal fuel and water for the said work" [Cox 1895, App. B, model contract].
  - Water had to be carted until the bore struck: "The item of cost of haulage of water long distances, for the engine alone for boring plants and for camp use, is a serious matter, which is only obviated on water being struck in the bore." [Cox 1895, p. 109]
  - **Gidgee** (*Acacia cambagei*) wood is "Hard, close-grained ... of a dark reddish or almost black colour". It is "a first-class firewood, being as good, if not better, than Mallee roots. The white ash is sometimes used for whitewash." The tree is also called "Stinking Wattle": "The smell of the tree is abominable, and just before rain almost unbearable" (the smell comes from the foliage). [Maiden, vol. 4, No. 117]
- **Blacksmith (tool dresser)**: runs the forge. He heats the dull bits, draws and sharpens them with sledges on the anvil, sizes them to the gauge, and repairs pole straps and jars [Cox 1895, p. 108 and App. C].
- **Cook**: "in the far away bush" [Cox]. A station manager's 1899 diary has a scrub-cutters' camp cook quitting: "I am tired of cooking; the meat is too poor, and there is no fat". [Press, 11 Sep 1899, reprinting the *Australasian Pastoralists' Review*. https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP18990911.2.10]
- **Contractor / boss**: bores were let by contract at so much per foot.
  - The best-known early contractor was J. S. Loughead of the Federal Boring Association. He sank Barcaldine and was described as "a big, blunt man from America" [Otago Daily Times, 8 Feb 1888, from the *Sydney Daily Telegraph*. https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/ODT18880208.2.38].
  - He came from North America with his own "apparatus". Cox says the Barcaldine tools came from Canada.
- **Squatter**: pays the contract and supplies wood, water, casing and cartage [App. B].
- **Paterson himself** lists the borers' camp among the fixtures of the bush in 1900: "From shearer's hut to artesian borers' camp, from the artesian well to the opal fields, from the opal fields to a gold rush". [*In No Man's Land*, ch. XIX, NZ Mail, 3 May 1900. https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZMAIL19000503.2.29]

**Minute to minute: the drilling cycle.**
1. **Drilling.** Engine running, band wheel turning, beam rocking 40-70 times a minute (Q1). The string rises and drops a foot and a half to three feet. The driller turns it a little now and then, and pays out chain as the bit cuts. In hard rock progress is "from one to six inches or even twelve inches of hard rock and shale per hour" [Cox 1895, p. 103]. Some bands were so hard that "sometimes requiring a week or more to penetrate even eight inches" [Cox 1895, p. 111].
2. **Pulling out.** After about a foot of rock [Cox 1895, p. 108] the beam is unhooked and the draw spool winds the string up. At each third joint the derrick man unscrews a stand while the big wrenches break the joint (App. C lists "knock wrenches" and "catch wrenches"; that knock wrenches were struck with a hammer is my inference from the name). A fork is pushed under each joint collar to hold the string at the mouth [Thompson 1910, p. 196]. The stands are racked in the derrick.
3. **Bailing.** The bailer goes down on the sand line, is churned at the bottom, is hauled up and dumped by standing it on its valve stem. This is repeated until the hole is clean.
4. **Change the bit** (the dull one goes to the forge). Run the string back in, reconnect, and drill again.
5. **Round-trip time grows with depth.** Boyd timed one round trip with wooden rods at 1,542 ft: drawing up the rods 25 minutes, bailer down 14, up 24, changing chisel 3, lowering 14, connecting the beam 5, total **85 minutes**. A rope rig did the same at 1,600 ft in about 20 minutes [Boyd 1894, p. 123]. At 3,000-4,000 ft a pole round trip would be several hours (my extrapolation, **UNVERIFIED**). That explains why the deepest Queensland bores finished on rope.
6. **Shifts.** 12 hours each, day and night if the contractor had two crews [Cox 1895, p. 110]. Hurricane lanterns and an engine-room lamp and reflector were part of the kit [App. C].

**Speed overall.**
- "Working twenty-four hours a day the rate of progress is from 5 ft. to 100 ft. per day" [Cox 1895, p. 110].
- At Barcaldine, "The sinking of the first 24 hours measured 110 feet, and in four weeks water was struck at a depth of 645 feet" [Ashburton Guardian, 14 Feb 1888. https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/AG18880214.2.27].

## Q4. When a bore struck

**The moment.** Barcaldine, December 1887, as reported in 1888 (the *Sydney Daily Telegraph*, reprinted):

- "No sign of water, brackish or otherwise, was seen all the way down, and the first intimation of its presence was obtained on Friday evening, Dec. 16th, when the drill suddenly dropped 7 feet. Within a few minutes the water ascended the bore and the memorable deluge of Barcaldine commenced. It rose several feet into the air, and then fell away in the form of a large glass dome."
- "Teamsters who had camped on ground lower than the mouth of the stream were flooded out."
- Sources: AG 14 Feb 1888 (above); ODT 8 Feb 1888 (above); NZ Times 29 Oct 1888, https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZTIM18881029.2.10
- Cox gives the strike date as 28 December [Cox 1895, p. 52]. 16 December 1887 was a Friday, so the newspaper date is internally consistent. **Treat the date as uncertain.**

**Controlling it.**
- "The water poured out to such an extent that the hydraulic engineer of Queensland endeavoured 'to turn it off,' but it was no use".
- "To stop the flow in the way first attempted was found impossible, each wooden plug being forced out as often as it was driven into the bore hole. A pipe 17ft long was then inserted into the bore and carried up to the top of the derrick ... and over the top of this the stream of water, 12in in diameter, has seemingly been allowed to run to waste." [ODT 8 Feb 1888]
- A year later: "to save the tubing from being shot out, the aperture had to be plugged and reduced" [NZH 14 Feb 1889].
- **Visual:** a dome of glassy water over the casing that nobody can plug. The heads were later fitted with pipes, goosenecks and tees.
  - Photos: gooseneck arc, Murweh 1897 (`ref/08`); horizontal spout, Saltern Creek 1899; dome over the casing, Tinnenburra 1891; column in the timber, Woolerina c. 1897 (`ref/07`); jet at the derrick floor, Charleville district (`ref/06`).

**The crowd.** "Within a few moments of the appearance of the stream the permanent and floating population of Barcaldine and its neighbourhood had gathered around the bore, and the intensity of the people's delight was evinced by their watching the outpour day and night. Everybody tasted the water ... It is as clear as crystal". [ODT 8 Feb 1888]

**Height of the jet.**
- Barcaldine: "several feet".
- Charleville: "the water rises with one leap for 100 ft in the air and pours itself out at the rate of 3,000,000 gal a day, under a pressure in the tube of 90lb to the square inch". This is from "Miss Shaw writing in *The Times*" (evidently Flora Shaw), reprinted in the Dunstan Times, 21 Apr 1893, https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/DUNST18930421.2.17. The 100 ft is probably journalistic (**UNVERIFIED**). The photos show free jets of roughly 10-30 ft, and domes where the casing is open at ground level.

**Flow ("a million-gallon flow" is realistic).** Figures are gallons per day, from Cox 1895, pp. 52-54 and App. A, unless noted.

| Bore | Flow |
|---|---|
| Barcaldine | 175,000 (early reports said 500,000-576,000) |
| Blackall | 300,000 |
| Cunnamulla | 540,000 |
| Charleville | 3,000,000 |

- Barenya and Leichhardt: 60 lb per sq in (SLQ captions, 1895).
- Charleville: 90 lb (Shaw 1893).
- Thargomindah: "as high as 230 lbs. to the square inch" [Cox 1895, p. 115].

**Temperature: deep bores were hot, some near boiling.**

| Bore | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Barcaldine (first reports) | about 120-126 F, falling to 102 F (the digits vary between OCR'd reprints) |
| Charleville | 106 F [Cox 1895, section plate] |

- Woolerina: "The water is hot for nearly a mile and a half from the bore, from which a surface drain has been made. It forms a beautiful clear stream" [Cox 1895, p. 95].
- Gregory on the South Australian bores (1901-02): "the water comes up hot and steaming. At the Mirra Mitta bore the water has a temperature of 190 degrees, and will make good tea without any further heating." [Gregory 1906, p. 55]
- **So steam over a deep, fast bore is true to life.** The Woolerina plate (`ref/07`) shows the column wrapped in haze.
- One 1892 newspaper claimed the water "is quite cold". The measurements above contradict it, so ignore it.

**Smell.** "it is admitted that many of the well-waters smell of sulphuretted hydrogen [rotten eggs], when they are first drawn" [Gregory 1906, p. 317, citing Pittman & David 1903]. Also: "it has been frequently remarked that the well-waters, when first drawn, smell unmistakably of sulphuretted hydrogen" [Gregory 1906, p. 329, citing Henderson's Hydraulic Engineer's report for 1896].

**Colour.** Clear. "clear, colourless, soft, and potable" (Charleville) [Cox 1895, section plate]; "as clear as crystal" (Barcaldine).

**Bore drains.**
- At Boatman station half the flow "has been carried by drains for thirty miles back. The drains are four feet wide and nine inches deep ... The sheep water at them without difficulty" [Cox 1895, pp. 54-55].
- QSA 1897 photos show a banked drain at Woolerina (`ref/09`) and a dam filled by drains.
- Cox 1906 shows cattle along the drain at Richmond Downs (`ref/10`).
- An 1899 station diary during drought: "Arrived at the bore ... find the bore drain blocked up by a woolly sheep ... having fallen in and got drowned" [Press, 11 Sep 1899].
- Overflow made lagoons: Barcaldine's "lagoon in which cattle wade up to their middle" [ODT 1888]; Murweh No. 3 "overflow lagoon" (QSA 1897).

**"And it's clear away the timber"** (interpretation; no source states what Paterson meant). Two readings fit the evidence:

1. **Trees.** Clear the scrub and trees so the drain can be cut and the water run. Paterson uses "timber" to mean trees two lines later ("By the silent belts of timber"). Many bores were in timbered country: Woolerina's plate shows the flow among eucalypts, with fallen logs lying in the flood (`ref/07`).
2. **The rig.** Pull down the derrick timbers once the plant moves on.

Against reading 2: at Barcaldine the derrick was *kept* at first to carry the outlet pipe, and the Charleville-district photo shows a derrick still standing over a flowing bore. **Reading 1 fits the stanza better.**

## Q5. Depths, times, costs, troubles

**Depths.**
- "the average depth of the bores in New South Wales and Queensland is about 1,300 feet" [Cox 1906, p. 99].
- Early Government bores [Cox 1895, p. 52 and App. A]:

| Bore | Depth |
|---|---|
| Barcaldine | 691 ft |
| Saltern | 978 ft |
| Tambo | 1,002 ft |
| Winton | 1,125 ft |
| Cunnamulla | 1,402 ft |
| Blackall | 1,663 ft |

- Deep bores of the poem's era:
  - Darr River Downs: water at 2,700 ft in 1891, carried to 3,630 ft, 500,000 gal [Cox 1895, p. 53].
  - Muckadilla: 3,262 ft, the colony's deepest in 1893 [Shaw 1893; strata log in Cox 1895].
  - Malvern Hills: 3,948 ft (see Q1).
  - **Bimerah**, while Paterson's poem was new: "It is down 4017 feet, and though water has not yet been struck the indications for a supply are promising. The drill is just now piercing a stratum of chocolate clay and sand" [Otago Witness, 21 Jan 1897, https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18970121.2.10; NEM 15 Feb 1897]. By 1903, "Bimerah they had to grope down to 5045 ft before they got what they wanted, but then they got a daily 70,000 gallons of it. This hole on Bimerah is still the deepest in Queensland" [WH 31 Oct 1903].
  - The poem's "passed three thousand feet ... four thousand feet below" is therefore exactly the frontier of 1896-97.

**Time.**
- Barcaldine: started 18 Nov 1887, struck in about four weeks at 645 ft, finished at 691 ft 9 in.
- Blackall: started Jan 1886, abandoned about 1,000 ft, restarted, finished at about 1,663-1,667 ft around April 1888 [Cox 1895, p. 52].
- Canadian practice in easy ground: 500 ft in a week (Petrolia) [Boyd 1894, p. 122].

**Costs.**
- NSW's first contracts: "Surface to 1000 ft. ... 27/- per foot. 1000 ft. to 1500 ft. ... 35/-. 1500 ft. to 2000 ft. ... 40/-, exclusive of tubing". The rate rises with depth, which is the poem's "contract price is growing".
- "The average cost of the early Queensland Government bores was about 37/- per foot."
- Barcaldine: 25/- a foot, "the Contractors being supplied with wood and water, and free rail and carriage".
- By 1895: "8/- to 15/- per foot, the Contractors finding everything excepting tubing and wood and water". [Cox 1895, pp. 128-129]
- Barcaldine total: "less than £700" [AG 14 Feb 1888; the OCR reads "i.700", taken here as £700].
- A complete rig "to drill 2000 ft. is from £1250 to £1600" [Cox 1895, p. 110]. NSW estimated the plant at "probably more than £2000".
- Payment was 25% progress payments per foot, none until 333 ft [App. B].

**Troubles.**
- **Caving** (the hole's sides falling in): tubes are needed "where the strata passed through consists of soft or loose material" [Cox 1895, p. 110]. Poles were favoured where "excessive caving makes it necessary to have some positive method of rotating the bit" [Thompson 1910, p. 193]. Quicksand was "the drillers' bugbear" [Cox 1895, p. 108].
- **Tubes jamming:**
  - Barcaldine's work "was impeded owing to difficulties in getting the necessary tubing for casing the bore when passing through sand or clayey formations" [ODT 1888].
  - Clay "would suck itself so close to the casing that very often it was difficult to move it" [Boyd 1894, p. 133].
  - Remedies: jacks, blocks and tackle on the draw spool, the walking beam, rams and clamps (Q2).
  - Cox also warns that with inferior iron "the screwed part will give way", the thread "drawing", which "may be thus ruin the bore" [Cox 1895, p. 117].
- **Rods bending and breaking:**
  - Heavy iron rods "vibrated with great force, and, striking against the sides of the bore, detached portions of loose rock" (the reason for light wooden poles plus jars) [Cox 1895, p. 107].
  - Boyd's Canadian wooden rods "broke very frequently, not through concussion, but in drawing up, because the clay had such an adhesive power on the tools and chisels at the bottom" [Boyd 1894, p. 139].
  - Rods also bent if the bit struck with the full falling weight of the string [Isler 1911, p. 156].
- **Stuck and lost tools**: jars and fishing tools. On rope rigs, "cases are of frequent occurrence where two and even three sets of tools have become fastened in a well" [Isler 1911, p. 148].
- **Failures:**
  - The Wellshot bore "sustained a series of accidents, and the site was abandoned in June, 1889" [Cox 1895, p. 52].
  - Reaching **granite** ended the contract: "should granite be struck at any depth then the said Contractor shall be paid for the depth ... and shall not be compellable to proceed further" [App. B].
  - One entry in the NSW Government bores list reads: "Bore determined, bed rock reached. This bore is outside recognised artesian water bearing formation" [Cox 1895, p. 50].

## What this means for the animation

### The rig, true to life
- **Derrick**: a tapering four-legged tower, 50-70 ft tall, about 20 ft square at the base and about 3 ft at the top.
  - Build it in timber or in thin angle-iron lattice. Both were used in 1890s Queensland, and the lattice iron ones read more "engineered".
  - A ladder runs up one leg to one or two small stagings, and to a crown block with two sheaves (hoisting rope and sand line).
  - The floor is planked on heavy sills. The bore mouth in the centre has a short pipe collar (the drive-pipe) sticking up.
- **Walking beam**: a single huge hardwood balk, about 26 ft long and 2 ft deep at the middle. It is pivoted on a 13 ft samson post that stands just outside the derrick.
  - The well end reaches into the derrick over the hole. The Canadian **spring pole** and feed chain hang there, and the chain comes down to a swivel on the top rod.
  - The far end is pinned by an iron **pitman** (connecting rod) to a crank on the **band wheel**. The band wheel is about 9 ft across, with spokes, and sits low under the beam end.
- **Engine**: a wheeled portable steam engine standing a few yards beyond the band wheel, with a belt about 20 ft long between them.
  - Firebox end toward the crew, tall black stack, big flywheel. The small engine pulley means **the flywheel spins several turns for every single stroke of the beam** (Boyd: band wheel about 5x the pulley). That gives a nice contrast: a blurred fast flywheel against a slow, heavy beam.
  - A cord (the "telegraph") runs from the throttle to a grooved wheel on a post by the driller.
  - The woodpile of split gidgee sits beside it: dark red-black logs that leave white ash.
- **Drums**: a hoisting drum and a sand-line drum on a timber frame beside the beam, each with a brake lever and a belt-tightener lever. **"Waiting at the lever for the word to let her go"** fits a man with his hand on the brake or belt lever of the draw drum, or the driller with the telegraph cord. Choose one; the sources do not name "the lever".
- **The string in the hole**: pale yellow wooden poles, 3-4 in thick and 16-20 ft long, each with black iron sleeves and screw joints at both ends. Below them hang the jars (two long flat steel links), then a heavy iron sinker bar, then the steel chisel bit. The **yellow rods** are real: Queensland yellow-wood boring rods.
- **Around the rig**:
  - Stands of three poles racked upright inside the derrick, or laid on the ground.
  - A pile of iron casing tubes on skids and coils of wire rope.
  - A forge with bellows, anvil, tongs and sledges, and bits lying about.
  - Water casks or a tank (water was carted in until the strike).
  - A 20 ft iron bailer leaning in the derrick, with a spoil heap of wet grey slurry.
  - Hurricane lanterns for night shifts.
- **Camp** a little way off: tents or canvas flies, a cook's fire, meat hung in a tree or on a pole (see the QSA camp photos).

### The drilling cycle, and the music
- Real Canadian rigs ran **about 40-70 blows a minute**. The stroke at the bit was **about 1½-3 ft**; the beam end moves roughly a foot and a half to two and a half feet.
- The song runs at 112.35 BPM. **One blow every two beats is 56 a minute, right in the true range.** One blow per beat (112 a minute) is about double real practice; that is fine as a stylised "drive her to the end" chorus, but know it is fast.
- The crank gives a smooth rocking motion. The *impact* is sharp because the bit falls within the slack of the jars and the spring pole, so a quick drop and small bounce at the bottom of each stroke reads correctly.
- **The driller's actions per stroke**:
  - Hand on the swivel at the top of the string.
  - At the top of each stroke (when the tools go weightless) he gives a small twist.
  - Every so often he tugs the slipper-out string to let out a little chain.
  - Occasionally he reaches for the telegraph cord to speed up or slow the engine.
  - Head down, feeling the blow come up through the wood.
  - Rope drillers sat on a high stool; a pole driller stands.
- **Every foot or so**: the beam is unhooked. The drum winds the rods up. The derrick man, high on the staging, catches each stand and unscrews it while men below break the joint with the big wrenches (possibly knocked round with a sledge; inference).
- Then the bailer goes down on its wire and is churned at the bottom. Up it comes streaming slurry, and it is dumped by knocking its valve on the ground. The bit is changed and the rods run back down.
- At depth this takes an hour and a half or more. **Most of a real bore crew's day is hoisting, bailing and changing bits, not hammering.**
- **John Henry beat, true to life**: sledge blows did not drive the bit, because the beam did that. Two true places for the driller's big sledge: **the blacksmith's anvil dressing a hot bit** (sledges, anvil and forge are in Cox's kit list), and, less certainly, **a knock wrench struck round to break a stuck joint** (the wrench is in the list; the hammering is inferred from its name). The storyboard's "sledge on the drill collar" in chapter E would be stylised. Moving it to the anvil or the joint wrench keeps the rhythm and is accurate.

### Trouble scenes (verse 4 and chapter F)
- **"The shaft has started caving"**: the hole's sides slump and sand runs in around the tools. The fix is to run casing down after the bit.
- **"Yellow rods bending in the water down below"**: long wooden strings whipping in a water-filled hole. Rods mostly broke when being *pulled* out of sticky clay.
- **"Tubes are always jamming"**: clay or sand grips the iron casing, especially after a stop overnight.
  - Men clamp heavy iron clamps round the pipe and hook on block and tackle to the draw drum, or pump hydraulic jacks under the clamps.
  - The engine labours: safety valve lifting, belt slipping and squealing, the beam frame creaking.
  - "Forty horse-power lift" means the engine being pushed past its rating on the draw drum.
- **"Hammer, splice and bully every snapping rod ... patch her up with fencing-wire"**: poles were joined and repaired with iron straps round the wood (Thompson). Fencing wire as a bush repair is plausible but **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Fishing**: the grab tools (spring grabs, sockets, hooks) go down to catch a lost bit.

### Striking water (chapters F/G)
- **The sign**: in hard ground the bit is pounding slowly. Then **the string suddenly drops**, as at Barcaldine where "the drill suddenly dropped 7 feet". The crew feel it before anything shows.
- **Then, within minutes**, water climbs the bore and bursts over the casing mouth: first a surging dome of glassy water ("a large glass dome"), then a solid column of several feet or more.
  - It is **clear**. If the bore is deep it is **hot and steaming**: 100-200 F at the head, near boiling on the deepest bores.
  - It smells of rotten eggs at first.
  - The site floods. Wooden plugs hammered in are blown straight back out. Everyone crowds round, tastes it, and watches all night.
- **The whistle**: portable engines carried a steam whistle, and Paterson's crew blow it. No source describes a whistle custom at strikes (**UNVERIFIED**), but it is period hardware.
- **Afterwards**: a pipe, gooseneck or tee is fitted to the casing head. The flow runs into a pool and out along a **bore drain**: a straight channel about 4 ft wide and 9 in deep with low earth banks, sometimes miles long, through timber and across the plain. Cattle and sheep water along it and lagoons form.

### Things in the current storyboard to reconsider
- **Water below granite (chapters F/G).** In the Great Artesian Basin the water is in porous **sandstone and "sand drift"** under blue shale, not under granite. Hitting granite meant failure, and the model contract let the contractor stop there.
  - A true-to-life "dive" can still do what the storyboard wants. Use the logged strata: yellow clay, black shale, grey pipeclay, "sand drift, white, and lignite", "shale, black, with streaks of coal", then white sandstone with water at the bottom. This is Muckadilla's log to 3,262 ft, 124 F [Cox 1895].
  - The last barrier can be a hard ironstone or "desert sandstone lined with iron bands" (Barcaldine), which the bit breaks through before it drops.
  - If the granite image is essential, treat it as the poem's metaphor, not geology.
- **Depth numbers.** Barcaldine struck at 645 ft and the average was about 1,300 ft; 3,000-4,000 ft was the 1896-97 record frontier (Bimerah). The ruler reaching about 4,000 ft is faithful to the poem and the period.
- **The Glasgow engine.** No evidence was found that boring engines came from Glasgow, and the "canny Scot" is Paterson's joke. The iron casing ("Stewart's") very probably did come from Glasgow. The "20 H.P." plate is realistic.
- **Driller's look.** A Canadian or American contractor-driller in a felt hat, flannel shirt, waistcoat and braces is on type. The hero's singlet is fine for heat. Most period photos show shirts with sleeves rolled.
